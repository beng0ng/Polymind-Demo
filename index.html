How to play
  • The match: one on one on a land-only 11×11 Drylands map, under the official 1v1 Might rules.
  • Win by taking Bardur's capital: move a unit onto it, then use Capture.
  • Stars arrive every turn (the +number). Spend them on techs, units and tile improvements.
  • Grow: move a unit onto a village and Capture it on your next turn. Fruit, hunting and farms add population; a full city levels up and gives you a reward.
  • Fight: select a unit, then tap a red target. The badge shows the damage you deal and what comes back.
  • Ruins (?) hold a random reward: a unit that starts its turn there can Explore.

Methodology

How PolyMind plays

PolyMind is a rules engine, a neural network that reads the board and proposes moves, and a short search that double-checks the moves that matter. Strength is measured by Elo on games the AI never trained on.

The target setting

PolyMind aims at one competitive format and tries to play it faithfully:

Mode
Might, one against one. You win by capturing the enemy capital.
Map
Tiny Drylands, 11×11 = 121 tiles, with no water.
Tribes
The AI plays Bardur, against any standard tribe. Legion+ is a separate model trained to play Oumaji.
Ruleset
Official_Rules_2: the official rules, minus Windmill, Sawmill, Forge or Market in places where they would do nothing. The current Elo ladder was played under Official_Rules_1.

A rules engine built from evidence

Polytopia has no public simulator, so the project wrote its own. It covers map generation, tribes, cities, economy, technology, units, combat, fog of war and victory. Every rule is recorded with its source and an evidence level, and a rule changes only when new evidence or an in-game check supports the change.

The browser version runs on a JavaScript port of the same engine. A test plays the Python and browser versions side by side and checks that they agree move for move.

What the AI sees and does

The network reads the board from one player's point of view, through the fog. Its input is 67 feature planes, one value per tile for things like terrain, units, cities and borders, plus a vector of global numbers such as stars and turn. The output is a score for every possible action. On the 11×11 map that is 30,641 actions. Illegal actions are masked out before a move is chosen, so the AI only ever picks legal moves.

The network

A residual convolutional network with 4 blocks of 64 channels. It has two outputs: a policy head that scores every action on every tile, and a value head that estimates how the game is going. The Legion models add a small head that estimates the probability of winning. Because the network is convolutional, it works on any map size.

Search on critical moves

Most moves come straight from the network. At critical decisions, such as several possible attacks, a Knight attack, or a capture with enemies nearby, the AI searches before it moves:

  1. Take the network's top candidate moves.
  2. Play out several continuations of the rest of its own turn after each one.
  3. Score each resulting position with the value head.
  4. Keep the network's own move unless another candidate scores clearly better.

Lookahead copies of the game cannot see through the fog, so the search never uses hidden information. A gate decides which positions are critical enough to search. Legion2, the strongest model, is Legion0 with a looser gate that searches more positions; it has no new weights of its own.

How strength is measured

  • Held-out maps. Map seeds reserved for final tests are never used for training or for choosing models.
  • Both seats. Each map is played twice, once from each side, so a lucky start cancels out.
  • Mirror tribes. Main ratings are Bardur against Bardur, so tribe strength doesn't skew them.
  • Elo with error bars. A Bradley–Terry fit over every game, with the scripted expert fixed at 1,000 and 95% ranges from bootstrap resampling.

See the benchmarks

Known limits

  • Training games stop at turn 60 as a draw. The real game has no turn limit.
  • Special tribes, water maps and peace treaties are not modelled.
  • Some mechanics rest on conflicting or unverified sources.
  • All training runs on CPU.

The stages

  1. Imitate a scripted expert

    A hand-written expert bot plays the game. The network first learns to copy its moves (behaviour cloning), then improves by playing itself with PPO, a standard reinforcement-learning method.

  2. Courses and drills

    Short lessons teach single skills: an economy and vision course, combat drills, and set scenarios.

  3. Population-based training

    Several networks train side by side with different reward styles. After each generation they play a tournament; the weakest are replaced by copies of the best. The winner became Champion, at 9.7 CPU hours.

  4. Exploiters and fine-tunes

    Specialist exploiters (a rush player and an economy player) are trained to find the main model's weak spots. The main line then trains against them: Rushbreaker, then Alpha1 at 13.9 hours, then Alpha2 and Alpha3.

  5. Search on top

    Adding lookahead search to Alpha1 gave Alpha1+, which beat the scripted expert in 144 of 150 games. A wider search gate and a win-probability head gave Legion0, and searching more positions gave Legion2, the strongest model and the default opponent here.

  6. Distilling search (in progress)

    AlphaPro tries to teach the network to play like the search without having to search. The first attempt did not beat Alpha1, so this work continues.

  7. Legion+: a second tribe

    Legion+ starts from Legion2's network and keeps training with PPO while playing Oumaji. It meets a mix of fixed models, the scripted expert, a random player and its own past selves. It has gained about 160 Elo as Oumaji over its starting point.

Why Polytopia

A whole strategy game in a short match

The Battle of Polytopia is a turn-based strategy game by Midjiwan. In one small match you grow an economy, research technology, expand and fight. That makes it a hard problem for AI that is still small enough to study without a data centre.

121tiles on the Tiny Drylands map PolyMind plays

Many decisions in one turn

A turn is not one move. Every unit can move and attack, every city can train, and you can research and build in any order. On the 11×11 map the AI chooses among 30,641 possible actions at every step, and only a few are legal at any moment.

You can't see the whole map

Fog of war hides the enemy until you explore. The AI has to scout, guess and plan with partial information, as a person does.

Economy, tech and war at once

Stars pay for everything. Spending on cities makes you richer later, and spending on units keeps you alive now. Good play balances long-term growth against short-term danger.

Exact combat, uncertain world

Combat follows a fixed formula, so an attack's outcome can be worked out in advance. The uncertainty comes from the map, the fog and the opponent. That suits lookahead search on the moves that decide fights.

A real competitive format

One-on-one Might on a tiny map is played competitively by people. That gives a clear goal and a fair way to compare the AI with human players.

Small enough for a laptop

An 11×11 map with short games means every model here was trained on CPUs. Research ideas can be tested in hours, not weeks.

The research problem

There is no official simulator or API. Before any learning, the rules had to be rebuilt and checked against the real game, so rules fidelity is part of the research. PolyMind measures its progress the same way throughout: Elo on held-out maps, from both seats.

Reading the numbers

  • A 100-point gap means the stronger model is expected to score about 64%; 400 points is about 91%.
  • Where two ranges overlap, the order of those models is not settled. Legion2 leads Legion0 by 26 points on 400 head-to-head games, inside both ranges.
